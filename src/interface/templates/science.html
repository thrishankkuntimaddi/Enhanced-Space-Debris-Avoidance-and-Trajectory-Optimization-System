The science inside the game

Every launch obeys real physics. Here are the equations.

SentinelFlight flies a real rocket through the real sky: the public catalog of more than 20,000 satellites and pieces of debris. Each section below says what a model means in plain words, gives the equation exactly as the code uses it, names the file it lives in, and states where it stops being true.

01Getting off the ground

How much a rocket can change its speed, and whether it can lift itself at all.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

Throwing mass out of the back makes you go faster. Burning a lot of fuel buys surprisingly little: the speed only grows with the logarithm of the mass ratio. That is why rockets have stages.

$$\Delta v = I_{sp}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad \Delta v_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k\,\in\,\text{stages}} I_{sp,k}\, g_0 \ln\frac{m_{0,k}}{m_{f,k}}$$
Used for the Δv budget in mission setupCode physics/vehicles.js · deltaVBudgetIdeal number: real flights lose 1.3–1.7 km/s of it (section 5)

Thrust, back-pressure and mass flow

An engine pushes harder in vacuum than at sea level, because air pressing on the nozzle exit pushes back. The engine burns propellant at a steady rate set by its vacuum efficiency.

$$F(h) = F_{vac} - \left(F_{vac} - F_{sl}\right)\frac{p(h)}{p_0}, \qquad \dot m = \frac{F_{vac}}{I_{sp,vac}\, g_0}, \qquad \text{TWR} = \frac{F_{sl}}{m_0\, g_0}$$
Code physics/flight.js · thrustAt, enginesLift-off needs TWR > 1 (setup screen shows it)

Launch azimuth on a rotating Earth

To reach an orbit tilted by \(i\) from a site at latitude \(\varphi\) you must head off at azimuth \(\beta\). The Earth's spin already gives you up to 465 m/s eastward, so the heading you actually fly is bent slightly to use it.

$$\sin\beta = \frac{\cos i}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad \beta_{rot} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(v_{orb}\sin\beta - \omega_E\, r\cos\varphi,\; v_{orb}\cos\beta\right)$$
Code physics/flight.js · launchAzimuthNo real solution when \(i < |\varphi|\): you cannot launch directly into an orbit less inclined than your latitude

02Air: atmosphere and drag

The first 100 km are the hardest: air pushes back, heats up and tries to bend the rocket.

U.S. Standard Atmosphere 1976

Temperature changes linearly inside each layer of the atmosphere. Pressure then follows from balancing the weight of the air above, and density from the ideal-gas law. Above 86 km the published density table is interpolated.

$$T = T_b + L\,(H - H_b), \qquad p = \begin{cases} p_b\left(\dfrac{T_b}{T}\right)^{g_0 / (R L)} & L \ne 0 \\[6pt] p_b\, \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{g_0 (H - H_b)}{R\, T_b}\right) & L = 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \rho = \frac{p}{R\,T}, \qquad a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$$
Geopotential height \(H = R_0 h/(R_0 + h)\), \(R_0 = 6\,356\,766\) mCode physics/atmosphere.jsA standard day: no weather, winds or solar activity

Drag, dynamic pressure and Mach number

Drag grows with the square of speed and with air density. Their product, the dynamic pressure \(q\), peaks about a minute after lift-off: that moment is Max-Q. Drag is computed against the air, which rotates with the Earth.

$$\vec v_{rel} = \vec v - \vec\omega_E \times \vec r, \qquad q = \tfrac12 \rho\, v_{rel}^2, \qquad \vec a_D = -\frac{q\, C_D(M)\, A}{m}\,\hat v_{rel}, \qquad M = \frac{v_{rel}}{a}$$

\(C_D\) follows a rocket-like Mach curve: 0.30 subsonic, rising to 0.64 just past Mach 1 (the transonic drag rise), then decaying to 0.28 at Mach 8.

03Gravity and Earth's shape

Earth is not a perfect sphere. Its equatorial bulge slowly twists every orbit.

Gravity with the J2 term

Point-mass gravity plus the largest correction for Earth's flattening (\(J_2\)). The game integrates this in the inertial TEME frame.

$$\vec a_g = -\frac{\mu}{r^3}\begin{pmatrix} x\left[1 + \tfrac32 J_2 \tfrac{R_E^2}{r^2}\left(1 - 5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}\right)\right] \\ y\left[1 + \tfrac32 J_2 \tfrac{R_E^2}{r^2}\left(1 - 5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}\right)\right] \\ z\left[1 + \tfrac32 J_2 \tfrac{R_E^2}{r^2}\left(3 - 5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}\right)\right]\end{pmatrix}$$
\(\mu = 3.986\,004\,418\times10^{14}\) m³/s²\(J_2 = 1.082\,626\,68\times10^{-3}\)\(R_E = 6\,378\,137\) mCode physics/orbit.js · gravity

Numerical integration: RK4

The rocket's motion is stepped forward with the classical 4th-order Runge–Kutta method. Steps are cut exactly at engine burnout so no stage burns a fraction of a step too long.

$$\vec y_{n+1} = \vec y_n + \frac{\Delta t}{6}\left(\vec k_1 + 2\vec k_2 + 2\vec k_3 + \vec k_4\right), \quad \vec k_1 = f(t_n, \vec y_n),\; \vec k_2 = f\!\left(t_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}, \vec y_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\vec k_1\right), \dots$$

04Flying the ascent

Rise vertically, tip over a few degrees, then let gravity do the turning.

Pitch kick and gravity turn

After a small kick toward the launch azimuth, the rocket keeps its nose pointed along its velocity (zero angle of attack) so the air never pushes it sideways. Gravity then bends the path toward horizontal on its own.

$$\dot\gamma = -\left(\frac{g}{v} - \frac{v}{r}\right)\cos\gamma, \qquad \alpha \approx 0 \text{ while } q \text{ is large}$$
Flight computer searches the kick angle with full ascent simulations before every launchCode physics/flight.js · KICK, GRAVITY_TURN

Closed-loop guidance and circularisation

Out of the air, guidance steers into a 200 km × target transfer orbit, coasts to apoapsis, then fires a circularisation burn. The cutoff speed comes from the vis-viva equation.

$$v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right), \qquad v_{\text{cutoff}} = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{2}{r + r_T}\right)}, \qquad \Delta v_{circ} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_T}} - v_{apo}$$
Code physics/flight.js · CLOSED_LOOP, CIRCULARIZE, cutoffDtResult all six vehicles reach their target within 3 % in altitude and 0.1° in inclination

05Where the Δv goes

The mission report shows this budget for every flight, and it always adds up.

Some of the engine's push is spent holding the rocket up against gravity, some fights the air, and some is wasted pointing slightly away from the direction of motion. What remains is the speed you gained.

$$\underbrace{\int \frac{F}{m}\,dt}_{\text{ideal}} \;-\; \underbrace{\int -\vec g\cdot\hat v\,dt}_{\text{gravity loss}} \;-\; \underbrace{\int -\vec a_D\cdot\hat v\,dt}_{\text{drag loss}} \;-\; \underbrace{\int \frac{F}{m}\left(1 - \hat u\cdot\hat v\right)dt}_{\text{steering loss}} \;=\; \Delta\lvert\vec v\rvert$$
Typical gravity loss 1.2–1.5 km/s, drag loss 50–150 m/sCode physics/flight.js · losses

06Orbits

Kepler's equation

Where a satellite is on its ellipse at a given time. The GPU solves this for every catalog object, every frame, to animate the whole sky.

$$M = M_0 + n\,(t - t_0) = E - e\sin E, \qquad n = \sqrt{\mu / a^3}$$

J2 secular drift

The equatorial bulge makes orbit planes rotate (nodal precession) and ellipses turn. Sun-synchronous orbits exploit exactly this, at about 98° inclination.

$$\dot\Omega = -\tfrac32 J_2 \left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2 n\cos i, \qquad \dot\omega = \tfrac34 J_2 \left(\frac{R_E}{p}\right)^2 n\left(5\cos^2 i - 1\right), \qquad p = a\,(1 - e^2)$$

07The catalog and SGP4

The real sky: public two-line element sets from Space-Track and CelesTrak, refreshed every 6 hours.

SGP4 propagation

Every object comes as a two-line element set (TLE): a compact description of its orbit at one moment. SGP4 is the standard model that turns a TLE into a position and velocity at any time. It includes Earth's shape, atmospheric drag (through the \(B^*\) term) and Sun–Moon effects for high orbits.

$$\text{TLE}\;\{n_0, e_0, i_0, \Omega_0, \omega_0, M_0, B^*\} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{SGP4}\;}\; \vec r(t), \vec v(t) \;\text{ in TEME}$$
Cross-check the browser's satellite.js against python-sgp4 (shown on the title screen); GMST matches to 1e-9 radCode workers/sgp4.worker.js; src/screening

How wrong is a TLE? A back-test

For 17,160 objects, the older element set was propagated to the epoch of the newer one and the two were compared: 25,840 pairs. The error grows with the age of the data, almost entirely along the track.

08Finding close approaches

About 2,500 objects screened against the rocket's path in 15–40 ms, in the browser.

Cubic Hermite interpolation

SGP4 is run every 60 s for every object (position and velocity). Between samples, a cubic curve that matches both is accurate to well under a metre, because orbital motion is very smooth.

$$\vec r(s) = h_{00}(s)\,\vec r_k + h_{10}(s)\,\Delta t\,\vec v_k + h_{01}(s)\,\vec r_{k+1} + h_{11}(s)\,\Delta t\,\vec v_{k+1}, \quad s\in[0,1]$$

Time of closest approach

Over one minute, two free-falling objects move almost in straight lines relative to each other, so the closest approach has a closed form. It is then refined on the exact curves with a golden-section search (agrees with brute force to 5 m and 0.05 s).

$$\Delta\vec r(t) \approx \Delta\vec r_0 + \Delta\vec v\, t, \qquad t^* = -\frac{\Delta\vec r_0\cdot\Delta\vec v}{\lVert\Delta\vec v\rVert^2}, \qquad d_{miss} = \lVert\Delta\vec r(t^*)\rVert$$

09Probability of collision

A miss distance alone says little. What matters is how sure we are of it.

The encounter-plane model

At closest approach, project everything onto the plane perpendicular to the relative velocity. The combined position uncertainty is a 2-D Gaussian there. A collision happens if the true miss is smaller than the combined hard-body radius (HBR) of the two objects. With equal uncertainty in both directions, the probability is a non-central chi-square distribution.

$$\sigma^2 = \sigma_{obj}^2(\text{age}) + \sigma_{veh}^2, \qquad P_c = \Pr\!\left[\chi'^2_{2}\!\left(\lambda = \tfrac{d_{miss}^2}{\sigma^2}\right) \le \tfrac{\text{HBR}^2}{\sigma^2}\right] = \sum_{j\ge0} \frac{e^{-\lambda/2}(\lambda/2)^j}{j!}\, P\!\left(j+1, \tfrac{\text{HBR}^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$
LevelRule
RED\(P_c \ge 10^{-4}\) or miss < 1 km: maneuver (the NASA CARA threshold)
AMBER\(P_c \ge 10^{-6}\) or miss < 5 km: watch
GREENeverything else

In the game, flying through a conjunction draws the outcome from its \(P_c\): a 1-in-1,000 risk really is one in a thousand. The vehicle's own uncertainty is \(\sigma_{veh} = 0.25\) km; the HBR comes from each object's radar cross-section.

10Dodging in orbit

Burns in the RTN frame

Avoidance burns are given as Radial (away from Earth), Transverse (along the motion) and Normal (out of the orbit plane). A tiny prograde push, made early, moves you a long way along the track by the time of closest approach. That is why early, small burns are cheap.

$$\hat R = \frac{\vec r}{r}, \quad \hat N = \frac{\vec r\times\vec v}{\lVert\vec r\times\vec v\rVert}, \quad \hat T = \hat N\times\hat R, \qquad \Delta s_{\text{along}} \approx 3\,\Delta v_T\, \Delta t \quad\text{(Clohessy–Wiltshire rule of thumb)}$$
Sentinel in orbit searches direction × size × lead time, then re-screens the best burn against the whole catalogCode game/main.js · btn-mv-ai; physics/frames.js · rtnBasis

11The Sentinel AI: Double DQN

Reinforcement learning that plans the launch, with a safety net it cannot remove.

The problem as a Markov decision process

The agent sees the current plan and the five most dangerous conjunctions. At each step it either commits or nudges the plan: launch 60 s earlier or later, insertion altitude ±2 km, or a phasing burn of ±0.5 m/s. Every step re-screens the plan against the real catalog, so it always acts on real physics, never on a learned guess.

$$s \in \mathbb R^{48}, \quad a \in \{\text{COMMIT}, \pm60\,\text{s}, \pm2\,\text{km}, \pm0.5\,\text{m/s}\}, \quad \Phi(s) = -\sum_i\left[\operatorname{clip}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{d_i}{5\,\text{km}}\right)^2 + 2\,\mathbb 1_{\text{RED},i}\right]$$
$$r = \Phi(s') - \Phi(s) \;-\; 0.25\,\Delta v \;-\; 0.1\,t_{slip,\min} \;-\; 0.02 \;+\; r_{\text{terminal}}$$

Shaping with a potential difference (Ng et al., 1999) speeds up learning without changing which policy is optimal.

Double DQN

A neural network estimates how good each action is. Plain DQN tends to overestimate, because the same network both picks and scores the next action. Double DQN lets the online network pick and the target network score.

$$y = r + \gamma\, Q_{\theta^-}\!\left(s',\, \arg\max_{a'} Q_\theta(s', a')\right), \qquad \mathcal L(\theta) = \operatorname{Huber}\big(y - Q_\theta(s,a)\big), \qquad \theta^- \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1-\tau)\,\theta^-$$
dueling head · 2 × 256 ReLU · Adam 3e-4 · γ = 0.97 · τ = 0.01 · action masks · 4,000 episodes (~8.5 min CPU)Served as numpy / plain JS: 13 µs per decision

What this shows: the guarded agent reaches 92 % of the brute-force optimum with 56× fewer screenings. In real operations a screening runs high-fidelity ephemerides against the whole catalog and takes minutes, so screenings are the cost that matters.

What it does not show: greedy search finds cleaner plans (93 % vs 82 %), and Double DQN ties vanilla DQN on such short episodes. The AI's honest value is speed, not better plans. Safety comes from the guard: every committed plan is re-screened, and if anything is still RED the system falls back to exhaustive search. The guard triggered in 0.5 % of cases.

12Honest limits

  • Public TLEs have no covariance. Uncertainty is modelled as isotropic and grows with data age. Real operators use owner-supplied ephemerides with full covariance, and the along-track error dominates.
  • Partial catalog without Space-Track credentials. The CelesTrak fallback omits most rocket bodies and small debris; the game labels it. Objects under ~10 cm are not tracked at all, yet can still destroy a satellite.
  • Approximate vehicles. Stage masses, thrust and Isp are public approximations, and there is no engine-out, wind or weather model.
  • The AI is not a certified tool. It was trained on simulated scenarios with injected threats. Its plans are always verified by physics, and it should be read as a research demonstration.
  • Simplified Pc. A 2-D Gaussian with a spherical hard body; no Monte Carlo for long, slow encounters.
  • Fragmentation events in missions are synthetic, drawn around real rocket bodies so they cross your orbit.

Take this further, together

SentinelFlight is an open, physics-true sandbox for learning how launches and space traffic really work. To grow into a tool that schools and operators can rely on, it needs partners:

  • Space agencies: real conjunction data messages and covariance, and validation against operational screening.
  • Universities: course material, student projects, and research on learned avoidance policies.
  • Satellite operators: maneuver constraints and ephemerides that make the planning problem real.
